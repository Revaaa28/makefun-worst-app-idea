# Candidates — The Worst App Idea Ever Pitched (u-plucky-sparrow-67-the-worst-app-idea-ever-pitched-a0396b23)

Judge brief, criteria in their published order:
1. Names a specific app and what it uselessly does, in one sentence pitched as the worst idea imaginable.
2. Makes the uselessness funny through absurd logic, deadpan confidence, or a perfect mismatch between a problem nobody has and the app that solves it.
3. Sounds like a real pitch, with a product name or a clear statement of what the app does and who it is for.
4. Uses the 240 characters well: one clean sentence, no padding, no second idea crowding it.
5. Leaves a vivid, specific picture of the app in the reader's head rather than a generic joke about apps.

Selection method: MANUAL ranking by hand against the brief, in the criteria's published order, plus pairwise checks against strong rival shapes. No Gemini judge, no NVIDIA judge, no API/proxy judge tournament was used. No judge-API calls for selection at all.

Gate: UTF-8, 1–240 characters, not only whitespace.

## The 12 candidates

1. (159 chars) "Introducing ToastTrack, the app that photographs your toast every morning, ranks it against 40,000 strangers' toast, and emails you a weekly toast report card."
2. (158 chars) "Introducing SockSync, the app that pairs your socks by personality type using a 40-question quiz, so no sock ever has to share a drawer with a stranger again."
3. (136 chars) "Meet Umbrella, the app that checks the weather for your umbrella every morning, so your umbrella is never caught in the rain unprepared."
4. (136 chars) "Introducing Napkin, the app that scans your napkin after every meal and tells you, with 94% confidence, whether you actually enjoyed it."
5. (155 chars) "Introducing Doorbell+, the app that rings your own doorbell for you, so you can finally hear what it sounds like from the inside without leaving the couch."
6. (142 chars) "Introducing SnoozePal, the app that texts your alarm clock a motivational quote every morning, so it feels appreciated enough to ring on time."
7. (148 chars) "Introducing CloudNap, the app that backs up your naps to the cloud, so you can restore last Tuesday's nap on any device, in 4K, with a progress bar."
8. (168 chars) "Introducing PocketEcho, the app that records your voice, plays it back to you half a second late, and charges $9.99 a month for the privilege of hearing yourself think."
9. (155 chars) "Introducing AirFresh, the app that sends your houseplant a daily affirmation text for $12 a month, because your fern has been looking a little down lately."
10. (144 chars) "Introducing ChairWatch, the app that counts how many hours your office chair sits empty and emails your boss a weekly chair-productivity report."
11. (171 chars) "Introducing FridgeFace, the app that lets your refrigerator rate your midnight snacking and sends your mother a weekly report, because someone has to hold you accountable."
12. (159 chars) "Introducing Queue, the app that lets you stand in a virtual line for concerts that already happened, so you can finally say you waited three hours for nothing."

## Manual ranking (by hand, against the brief in order)

**Rank 1 — #1 ToastTrack (159 chars). WINNER.**
- Criterion 1: names a specific app and its useless function, elaborated in three concrete beats (photographs toast, ranks it, emails a report card) — the uselessness is the whole product, not a side gag.
- Criterion 2: absurd logic at full strength — toast treated as a competitive sport with a 40,000-person leaderboard and a report card; deadpan founder certainty in "weekly toast report card".
- Criterion 3: reads exactly like a real pitch ("Introducing ToastTrack, the app that...").
- Criterion 4: one clean sentence, no padding, no second idea; 159 chars uses the budget well.
- Criterion 5: the most vivid picture of the twelve — a toast leaderboard and a report card in the reader's head.

**Rank 2 — #2 SockSync (158 chars).**
Same pitch skeleton and nearly as strong: a 40-question personality quiz for socks, "share a drawer with a stranger" is a genuinely funny line. Loses to ToastTrack on criterion 5: the toast leaderboard + report card leaves a sharper, more specific picture than a quiz + a drawer, and "ranks it against 40,000 strangers" is a more absurd scale than one quiz.

**Rank 3 — #7 CloudNap (148 chars).**
"Restore last Tuesday's nap on any device, in 4K, with a progress bar" is the best single deadpan tech detail of the twelve, and the cloud-backup parody is vivid. Loses to ToastTrack on criterion 2: cloud backup is a real product pattern applied to a silly object, while competitive toast ranking is a problem nobody has ever had, solved with total seriousness — the mismatch is sharper.

**Rank 4 — #5 Doorbell+ (155 chars).**
Perfect mismatch (ringing your own doorbell to hear it from the inside) and very clean, but it is a one-beat joke; ToastTrack's three-beat function beats it on criteria 1 and 5.

**Rank 5 — #6 SnoozePal (142 chars).**
Personifying the alarm clock ("feels appreciated enough to ring on time") is strong absurd logic, but the motivational-quote mechanism is slightly softer than ToastTrack's leaderboard, and the picture is thinner.

**Rank 6 — #3 Umbrella (136 chars).**
The cleanest mismatch of all (weather forecast for your umbrella), deadpan and short. Loses on criterion 4 — 136 chars leaves budget unused — and on criterion 5: one beat, no scene beyond the umbrella.

**Rank 7 — #4 Napkin (136 chars).**
"With 94% confidence, whether you actually enjoyed it" is excellent deadpan confidence, but the mechanism (a napkin knows if you enjoyed your meal) is muddier than the others — the absurd logic needs a half-second of work from the reader.

**Rank 8 — #12 Queue (159 chars).**
"Stand in a virtual line for concerts that already happened" is a strong mismatch and "waited three hours for nothing" lands clean. Loses to ToastTrack on criterion 1: the app's function is the line itself, a thinner product than ToastTrack's three-part machine.

**Rank 9 — #8 PocketEcho (168 chars).**
"$9.99 a month for the privilege of hearing yourself think" is a good pricing punch, but the delayed-echo gag is a slightly familiar joke shape, and it reads more like a joke about a feature than a founder pitching a product.

**Rank 10 — #9 AirFresh (155 chars).**
Warm absurdity (a fern "looking a little down") and vivid, but houseplant-affirmation is a familiar gag shape, and it leans on cuteness rather than deadpan founder certainty.

**Rank 11 — #10 ChairWatch (144 chars).**
Chair-productivity report emailed to the boss is funny, but the surveillance angle is a second flavour crowding the pitch, and "counts empty chair hours" is a weaker core function than the top three.

**Rank 12 — #11 FridgeFace (171 chars).**
"Sends your mother a weekly report" is the most familiar punchline shape of the twelve (tattling to mum), and the fridge-rating mechanism is less specific than the top entries; weakest on criterion 2's originality.

## Pairwise checks (winner vs the strongest rivals)

- **ToastTrack vs SockSync:** both name an app + useless function in one clean sentence. ToastTrack wins on criterion 5 (leaderboard of 40,000 strangers' toast + weekly report card = two concrete images; SockSync has one image, the drawer) and on criterion 2 (a competitive ranking for toast is a stranger problem to solve than mismatched socks).
- **ToastTrack vs CloudNap:** both parody real product patterns. ToastTrack wins on criterion 2: nobody has ever wanted their toast ranked, while backing things up to the cloud is a normal behaviour — CloudNap's absurdity is one step removed, ToastTrack's is direct.
- **ToastTrack vs Doorbell+:** Doorbell+ is cleaner, but ToastTrack wins on criteria 1 and 5 — a three-part product (photograph, rank, report) against a one-trick product, and a fuller picture.

## Winner

> Introducing ToastTrack, the app that photographs your toast every morning, ranks it against 40,000 strangers' toast, and emails you a weekly toast report card.

159 characters. One sentence. No second idea, no padding, no shock value.
